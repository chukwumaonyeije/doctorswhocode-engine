I walked into clinic and heard a patient quote a viral number about “13,000 migrant homicides this year.” Minutes later I was reading a YouTube transcript alleging a pay‑to‑post political machine pushing the same narrative.

This is not culture‑war gossip. It is an operations problem. And it bleeds directly into health policy, clinical trust, and our workflow.

I am a physician who writes code. When I see coordination without disclosure, I see an attack surface.

- What this is

Payments are routed through consulting firms, obscuring end recipients, sidestepping disclosure because posts push “issues” not goods.

## Physician-developer takeaway

Health information rides the same rails as political messaging. If scripted, undisclosed influence can steer a base toward a vote, it can also steer patients away from vaccines, prenatal care, or palliative truth. This is an architecture problem.

## What physicians should build now:
- Content provenance by default
  - Ship C2PA or similar cryptographic signatures on health org media and require it in our collaboratives. No signature, no repost from official channels.
- An open claims ledger for health policy
  - Public, append‑only records that map a claim to its source, funding if known, first-seen timestamp, and current evidentiary status. Make it machine‑readable.
- Synchrony detection and rapid response
  - Time‑series clustering to flag synchronized bursts about clinical topics. Auto‑open a ticket with a 150‑word evidence card and a named physician owner.
- Disclosure as culture, not compliance
  - Medical societies should adopt a simple rule: if a member is paid to post about a policy or product, say it plainly on the post. No exceptions.
- Patient‑facing clarity cards
  - One screen. The claim, what the best evidence says, and a next step. Publish under a permissive license so community clinics can reuse.

## Measure what matters:
- Time to clarification from burst detection to first clarity card.
- Falsehood half‑life in your patient portal and call logs.
- Provenance coverage rate for official media.
- Script reuse index across posts about the same claim.
- Patient deflection rate from rumor to visit or evidence page.

## Critical flags

- Partisan heat, comedic framing, and personal feuds increase noise. Expect selection bias.
- Allegations about named individuals are not evidence. Do not repeat them as facts.
- Transcript-only access means we may miss visual receipts, on‑screen citations, or disclaimers.
- The most actionable portion is the system description of coordination and disclosure gaps. Focus there.

## Recommended next step

For builders:
- Stand up a minimal claims ledger in a weekend sprint. Data model: claim_id, text, topic, first_seen_url, provenance_type, funding_path_known (y/n), evidence_links, status, owner, timestamps. Publish an API.
- Add a synchrony detector. Start simple: cosine similarity on text plus time‑bucket clustering. Flag bursts over a threshold.
- Wire a bot to post a draft clarity card in your comms Slack whenever a burst touches your service lines.

## For governance:
- Draft a one‑page policy for your org: disclose paid advocacy in social posts about health policy, even if not legally required. Publish it. Model the culture you expect.
- Ask your medical society to endorse content provenance standards for all official media this year.

## For clinical ops:
- Build a rotating “What we’re hearing online” tile in the patient portal. One claim per week. Short, sourced, and calm.

## Conclusion:
We cannot code away bad faith, but we can raise the cost of coordination and lower the time to clinical clarity. If we do not build the rails for provenance, others will build the portals for persuasion. I choose to build.
