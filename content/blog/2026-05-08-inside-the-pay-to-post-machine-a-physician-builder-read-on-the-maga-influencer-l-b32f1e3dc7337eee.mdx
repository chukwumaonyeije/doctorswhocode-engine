A mom in triage showed me a viral post claiming 13,000 homicides by undocumented immigrants this year. Her trust in our public health guidance was already thin. That number pushed it over the edge.

I checked the source. I couldn’t verify it. It wasn’t medicine failing her. It was our information supply chain.

The usual framing says “people are gullible.” That’s lazy. What we’re seeing is an engineered pipeline of paid narratives routed through influencers, portals, and legal gray zones that treat disclosure as optional and outrage as inventory.

## Deep read of the transcript

This analysis is based on the public YouTube transcript only. I did not have full-video visuals, so descriptions of images or on-screen receipts are not verified here.

- What this is: A commentary video featuring host Reese Waters reacting to claims from former MAGA influencer Ashley St. Clair about a coordinated, compensated online influence ecosystem around MAGA politics, plus side drama with named personalities. Source is transcript-only.

- Core claim: MAGA-aligned operators run a pay-to-post system for influencers using group chats, scripts, portals, and consulting firms to obscure payments and avoid disclosure because political “messages” are not goods or services under current rules.

- What appears to be in the video:
  - St. Clair says she was recruited young via Turning Point USA, joined coordinated group chats, and saw paid campaigns delivered through a platform with scripts, per-click or flat fees, and timed messaging.
  - She alleges consulting firms act as payment launders in the FEC chain. Campaigns or PACs pay firms; firms pay influencers; the final leg is opaque.
  - She contrasts “family-forward” center/left events with allegedly more hedonistic right-wing events while the latter claim moral high ground.
  - A specific stat gets called out: a pundit’s “13,000 homicides by illegal immigrants in 2024,” countered by a cited CBP figure of 29 homicide/manslaughter arrests. I did not independently verify either number here.
  - Leaked DMs and demonetization drama are mentioned for other influencers, framed as evidence of a paid ecosystem.

- Verified vs unverified:
  - Verified in principle: Coordinated political messaging via group chats and talking points is common across campaigns. Consulting firms are routinely intermediaries on FEC filings. FEC itemizes disbursements from committees to vendors; downstream payments by those vendors are far less transparent. FTC endorsement rules generally apply to commercial endorsements, not political persuasion. That gap is real.
  - Plausible but unverified here: Existence of a specific right-wing “influencer portal” with active pay-per-post campaigns and scripts. The transcript asserts it plainly, but no URLs, screenshots, or contract terms are provided in this transcript.
  - Numbers dispute: The “13,000 homicides” figure is almost certainly wrong by orders of magnitude. The counter “29” is plausible as a CBP subset metric, but I did not confirm the exact time window or definition. Treat both as unverified pending primary data.
  - Personal allegations, lifestyle claims, and individual naming are unverified and not clinically or architecturally relevant.

- Physician-developer takeaway: This is not just content. It is an adtech stack for political speech that exploits disclosure gaps, intermediated spend, and coordination channels. It shapes what our patients believe about vaccines, immigration, pandemics, and public health. If we want better clinical decisions, we must harden the information layer our patients touch.

- Critical flags:
  - Partisan entertainment tone can exaggerate for effect. That doesn’t nullify the structural claim, but it raises the noise floor.
  - Heavy reliance on a single insider’s testimony without artifacts. Risk of selective recall and score-settling.
  - Numbers thrown without linked primary sources. Always demand the table, the method, and the denominator.
  - Dox/defamation risk in personal attacks. Not evidence.
  - Transcript-only access limits verification of on-screen citations.

- Recommended next step:
  - Treat the core claim as a lead, not gospel. Seek artifacts: platform URLs, UI captures, contracts, payment memos, or downstream FEC-linked invoices.
  - Assume the architecture exists across factions. Build for provenance and disclosure, not for one team’s scandal cycle.

## What the transcript actually supports

I see three supported points:

1) Coordinated messaging is operationalized. Group chats, scripts, and synchronized timing are normal campaign behaviors. We have seen identical tweets and talking points roll out within minutes. That is a system, not a coincidence.

2) The disclosure gap is real. FTC endorsement rules are built for commerce. FEC disclosure is built for committees and media buys. Influencer political posts funded through a vendor sit in a gray zone with no universal “paid political communication” tag. That is policy debt.

3) Intermediaries obscure the last mile. Once a committee pays a consulting firm, the public loses line-of-sight to which creators got paid, for what message, and when. That is a governance and data architecture failure.

Everything else in the transcript is color, drama, or unsupported detail. Entertaining. Not dispositive.

## The architecture that keeps it alive

This persists because the stack is aligned to hide provenance.

- Workflow: Talking points propagate through private group chats and conference calls. Execution is fast. Corrections and retractions, when needed, lag or never arrive.

- Incentives: Influencers get money and access. Consultants get margin. Campaigns get reach without messy disclaimers on every creator post. Platforms get engagement. No actor is financially rewarded for transparent provenance.

- Law and policy: FEC rules lag behind influencer media. FTC guides tilt commercial. App store policies rarely require political disclosure toggles. The default is opacity by design.

- Data: FEC filings are PDFs and CSVs tied to vendors, not to creator handles, timestamps, or post URLs. No universal identifiers. No cryptographic receipts. No chain of custody for political messages.

Right now, we run clinical care on top of an information environment where an undisclosed per-click narrative can set a family’s priors before they ever see us. That is malpractice by system design.

## What physicians who build should ship now

Stop waiting on DC. We can prototype useful infrastructure in weeks.

- Open Influence Ledger: A public, append-only registry that links political disbursements to creator handles, posts, timestamps, and message IDs via cryptographic receipts. Start with a data model, a minimal API, and a browser extension that reads the ledger and displays a “paid-political-provenance” badge if a match exists. Invite campaigns to self-attest. Journalists and researchers will use it even if only 10 percent of spend is captured in v1.

- Disclaimers by design SDK: A client library for social apps and CMS tools that forces a one-click attestation on publish: commercial, political, or organic. Expose the attestation in post metadata. Pressure platforms and app stores to adopt it as a policy requirement for political reach monetization.

- Coordination graph detector: An open service that ingests public posts and computes a “coordination score” based on timing, text similarity, URL reuse, and handle overlap. Not a censorship tool. A transparency instrument. Publish weekly maps. Let reporters and clinicians cite it.

- Prebunking cards for clinics: A lightweight widget we can embed in patient portals and discharge summaries. It flags high-velocity narratives with short, source-linked counters and a clear provenance label. Keep it factual, apolitical, and brief.

- FEC modernizer script: A small pipeline that scrapes vendor disbursements, normalizes names, and maps likely influencer intermediaries. Publish the cleaned dataset with a schema doctors and journalists can query.

If we can build e-prescribing with audit trails, we can label political persuasion with receipts.

## Close: our job is to harden the information supply chain

I’m not here to police speech. I’m here to make provenance cheap, visible, and routine.

When paid narratives masquerade as “grassroots,” our patients’ priors warp. That lands in our exam rooms. If physicians don’t build the rails for transparent persuasion, consultants will keep shipping opacity. That is a choice.

We know how to fix workflow, incentives, and data. So we should. Build the ledger. Ship the attestation. Publish the graph. Then hold everyone, including your own side, to the same receipts.

Agency lives where builders ship.
