At 2 a.m. a dad waved a viral thread in my face as proof his wife didn’t need Rhogam. He didn’t cite a study. He cited a blue‑check.

That moment was not about evidence. It was about an influence supply chain we pretend doesn’t exist in healthcare.

If politics has industrialized pay‑to‑post propaganda, medicine should stop acting shocked when the same playbook shapes patient belief. We need to build for it.

## What this is

A deep analysis of a YouTube video titled “Maga's Dirty Laundry Is As UGLY As It Gets” based on its transcript only. No access to the full video or independent source docs. The host amplifies and reacts to claims from former MAGA influencer Ashley St. Clair about coordinated, compensated online campaigns.

I am treating this as an anatomy lesson in influence operations, not a referendum on left vs right. The architecture is the point.

## Core claim

According to the transcript, MAGA online messaging functions like a professional marketing program with:
- group chats coordinating talking points and timing
- scripted posts and campaign briefs
- a portal where influencers pick campaigns and get paid per click or per post
- pass‑through consulting firms that obscure who pays whom, exploiting disclosure gaps since “issue advocacy” is not a product ad

The result is industrialized narrative shaping with no required “paid” label.

## Physician‑developer takeaway

Stop treating misinformation as a content problem. It is a workflow, incentive, and disclosure problem. The fix is systems.

Here is what I would build and deploy in clinics, societies, and platforms within 90 days:
- Open influence disclosures for health accounts
  - A simple, machine‑readable med-coi.json standard linked in bio that lists funding, consulting, speaking, affiliate links, and political issue contracts
  - Auto‑badge in clinician directories and conference apps when present; flag when absent
- Campaign‑similarity detection
  - Daily clustering of public posts on X, TikTok, YouTube, Instagram using n‑gram and embedding similarity with near‑time alignment
  - Output a “campaign similarity index” and a “probable script” snippet; notify moderators and show readers a small banner: “High similarity to coordinated messaging”
- Probable‑pay indicator for issue advocacy
  - Heuristics: identical CTAs, shared UTM codes, synchronized links to petitions, and overlap with known consulting firm link trees
  - Score posts 0–100 for pay‑likelihood; require a “paid/affiliated” toggle in clinician creators’ tools when score exceeds threshold
- Consent‑quality prompts for patients
  - In patient portals and discharge instructions, add a one‑click “How to evaluate medical posts” card: source, study, stake
  - Train front‑desk and nursing to ask three questions when a patient cites social media: Who paid? What study? What risk if wrong?
- Society‑level governance
  - Adopt a disclosure by default policy for all member social content
  - Publish a quarterly transparency report: top narratives, detectable campaigns, response time, and corrections issued

This is not censorship. It is labeling, provenance, and friction placed at the spread points where it matters.

## Critical flags

- Disclosure loophole: Issue advocacy exploits a regulatory gap. That gap will swallow health policy debates and public health guidance if we ignore it.
- Pass‑through opacity: Once money moves to a consulting firm, public traceability ends. Expect the same model for anti‑vax and supplement pushes.
- Synchronized scripting: Identical phrasing can outcompete peer‑reviewed nuance in the feed. Our current academic cadence is too slow.
- Platform incentives: Virality rewards outrage, not disclosure. Without external standards, creators will not self‑label paid narratives.
- Clinic blast radius: Frontline teams absorb the confusion. Every viral falsehood becomes a 20‑minute room delay.

## Recommended next step

- Ship a minimal, public “Campaign Similarity Monitor” for health topics
  - Data: RSS from major platforms’ search endpoints, crowdsourced link submissions
  - Methods: MinHash + cosine similarity on text, link de‑duplication, cross‑post timing windows
  - Output: a daily JSON feed of clusters with example posts, CTAs, and a similarity score
  - License it permissively and invite societies, health systems, and journalists to consume it
- Convene three pilot adopters
  - One specialty society adds med-coi.json to member profiles
  - One health system tests the patient three‑question script
  - One EHR adds a patient‑facing “Evaluate this claim” micro‑module to after‑visit summaries
- Publish a short governance memo
  - Ask the FTC and FEC to require “paid” labels for issue advocacy posts tied to health and safety
  - Until that happens, we hold ourselves to a higher bar and make non‑disclosure visible

I am not waiting for platforms or agencies to rescue clinical reality. We built medicine on provenance and accountability. We can build that into our information layer too.

Physicians who code have agency here. If we do nothing, undisclosed campaigns will keep practicing medicine in our exam rooms. If we build, we set the standard and force everyone else to catch up.
